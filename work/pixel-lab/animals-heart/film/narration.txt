A blue whale's heart can slow to just two beats a minute. A shrew's heart can beat twenty-five times a second. Mostly, size sets the speed. On a deep dive, the whale's heart beats once every thirty seconds. Its huge, stretchy main artery keeps the blood moving between beats. But a heart can also slow down to save energy. A groundhog's heart beats about eighty times a minute. In winter it hibernates, a deep sleep that lasts for months. Its heart drops to about five beats a minute, so its fat lasts until spring. A resting sea turtle's heart beats about eleven times a minute. It can sleep underwater for hours. An elephant's heart beats about thirty times a minute. Yours beats about seventy. A cat's beats about a hundred and thirty at home. At the vet, it jumps to almost two hundred. Smaller bodies lose heat faster. So they burn food faster, and the heart must pump faster. A rabbit's heart beats about two hundred and fifty times a minute. A mouse's beats about six hundred, ten every second. A hummingbird's can race past twelve hundred. And the record goes to the Etruscan shrew: up to fifteen hundred beats a minute. Now the twist. Many mammals get roughly a billion heartbeats in a whole life. A fast mouse lives about two years. A slow elephant can live about sixty. Both get close to a billion. We get about three billion. And the biggest heart on Earth?
