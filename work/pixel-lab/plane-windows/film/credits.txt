Why airplane windows are round — Kagenami pixel film (2026-10-24)

Voice: ElevenLabs, Dennis · Eleven v4 (one take); word timings checked with ElevenLabs Scribe.
Picture and sound: generated locally (pixel art and 8-bit synthesis). The airliner, the 1950s jet in the flashback and the
window cross-section are our own illustrations: not a real aircraft or airline, not to scale; the stress lines and the
crack are a simplified picture of metal fatigue. The channel's character: the creator's own pixel sprite sheet.

Sources:
- De Havilland Comet: the first jet airliner; two Comets broke up in flight in 1954 (BOAC Flight 781, January; South
  African Airways Flight 201, April). The investigation found metal fatigue: a crack that started at the corner of a
  window cut-out in the roof grew with repeated pressurisation until the cabin tore. Later airliners use rounded windows.
- Window panes and the breather hole: airliner cabin windows have an outer pane, a middle pane with a small bleed hole and
  an inner scratch pane; the hole equalises the pressure so the outer pane carries the load and the middle pane is a
  backup, and it vents moisture against fogging — Boeing's Bret Jensen and GKN Aerospace, as quoted by Mark Vanhoenacker
  (Slate) and TIME (2015).
