Ever tried to tap your phone with gloves on? You tap. Nothing. You press harder. Still nothing. The phone isn't frozen, and the glass isn't broken. Your screen simply doesn't feel pressure. It feels electricity. Under the glass sits a grid of invisible electrodes, rows and columns about five millimetres apart. The phone pulses the rows, and at every crossing a small electric field bulges up through the glass. Your body is mostly salty water, so it conducts. When your fingertip comes close, it pulls part of that field into you, and the crossings beneath it read less charge. A chip checks every crossing over a hundred times a second and maps the dip. Your fingertip covers several crossings, so the phone finds the centre of the dip, finer than the grid itself. Wool doesn't conduct and keeps your skin off the glass, so the field barely reaches you and the map stays flat. Touchscreen gloves weave silver thread into the fingertips to carry the field to your skin. And in one freezing Korean winter, people used snack sausages instead. Salty water, just like you.
