You press play, and the show starts in about a second. Ever wondered how Netflix pulls that off for over three hundred million homes? The menus and recommendations run in Amazon's cloud. But the video itself comes from somewhere much closer. Netflix built its own delivery network: more than eighteen thousand of its own servers, sitting inside internet providers all over the planet, the bigger ones holding over a hundred terabytes each. Every night, while the internet is quiet, these boxes download what people nearby will probably watch tomorrow. So when you press play in London, your episode usually comes from a box a few kilometres away, not from California. If that box is busy, the app quietly switches to the next one, say, in Amsterdam. And it isn't one file. The UK catalogue alone has almost nine thousand titles, and every one is stored in many versions: different sizes, codecs for different screens, dozens of audio tracks and subtitles. One season of Stranger Things became almost ten thousand files. Each version is cut into chunks a few seconds long, and your player picks every next chunk to fit your connection. That's why the picture sharpens a moment after it starts. Altogether, Netflix has been about fifteen percent of all the data flowing down the internet. Want more breakdowns like this?
