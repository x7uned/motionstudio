A wet dog can fling off about seventy percent of the water in its fur in just seconds. It isn't fussiness, it's physics. Water clings to every hair, and drying by evaporation is expensive: a sixty-pound dog carrying one pound of water would burn about a fifth of its daily calories just to dry off. So instead, it spins the water away. A Labrador twists back and forth about four and a half times a second. Its spine only turns so far, but its loose skin whips around three times wider, so every droplet gets flung off the fur. Across the animals researchers filmed, that spin pulls on the water with ten to seventy times the force of gravity. And here's the twist: smaller animals have to shake faster. A droplet on a mouse travels a much tighter circle, so a mouse shakes about thirty times a second. A rat, around eighteen. A bear, just four. Researchers at Georgia Tech filmed thirty-three wet mammals and found one rule: the smaller the animal, the faster the shake.
